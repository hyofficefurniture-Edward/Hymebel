EAC TR CU 025/2011 Furniture Certification: Buyer's Guide

Hymebel Editorial Team · Hongye Furniture Group · 2026-09-30 · Updated 2026-10-02 · 7 min

EAC TR CU 025/2011 is the mandatory technical regulation of the Eurasian Customs Union covering furniture safety for markets including Kazakhstan, Russia, Belarus, Armenia and Kyrgyzstan. Furniture imported into these member states must carry an EAC certificate or declaration of conformity before customs clearance.

Key takeaways
  • TR CU 025/2011 is mandatory for furniture: without conformity documents, goods do not cross the Customs Union border.
  • The EAC mark is supported either by a certificate (serial production) or a declaration of conformity.
  • Test reports come from accredited laboratories; the certificate or declaration itself is issued by a certification body.
  • Labeling must be in Russian and Kazakh — the requirement most often forgotten by first-time importers.
  • Hymebel holds 8 international certifications and produces regularly to EAC requirements.

What is EAC TR CU 025/2011?

TR CU 025/2011 is the technical regulation governing furniture placed on the market of the Customs Union framework — Russia, Belarus, Kazakhstan, Armenia and Kyrgyzstan. It sets safety requirements for materials, mechanical strength and emissions such as formaldehyde, and it is mandatory: furniture without conformity documents does not clear customs.

The visible result is the EAC mark on the product and its documentation. Behind it stands either a certificate of conformity (for serial production) or a declaration of conformity, supported by test reports from accredited laboratories.

The regulation belongs to a family: TR CU 025 sits alongside the technical regulations for machinery, wood and materials that together define what may legally be sold inside the union. For a furniture buyer this matters practically — the certificate must reference the correct regulation and product group, and test reports must map to its safety clauses. Generic quality certificates carry no weight at the border.

Hymebel has produced furniture on its 300,000 m² own factory since 1996 and ships regularly into EAC markets, which is why this guide reads the regulation the way a manufacturer does — from the loading dock, not just the legal text.

Who needs the EAC mark — and when?

Any manufacturer or importer bringing furniture into an EAC member state needs it: hotel FF&E batches bound for Kazakhstan fall squarely within scope. Responsibility formally sits with the importer, but in practice buyers require the factory to hold or obtain the documents, because a shipment stopped at the border costs the project far more than certification itself.

Note the geography: EAC applies to Customs Union members such as Kazakhstan. Shipments to Uzbekistan, which is outside the EAC, follow national import rules instead — one more reason to confirm requirements per destination before production starts, not after containers are loaded.

Timing matters as much as scope. Serial-production certificates are the efficient route for factories that ship repeatedly, while one-off project batches can run under batch schemes tied to the contract. The choice affects cost, validity and how quickly a replacement shipment can move, so agree it with the factory before the production window opens, not after samples are approved. EAC paperwork is what lets a hotel furniture batch cross that border without stopping.

Check three things before shipping: that test reports cover the correct product group, that labeling languages are ready, and that the certificate remains valid through the expected arrival date. Each line item in the table is cheaper to fix at month one than at the border.

Two notes on this table. First, schemes differ: a serial certificate covers a product line for its validity period, while batch certification ties documents to a single shipment and contract — your freight cadence decides which is cheaper. Second, originality counts: scans in a chat are useful for coordination, but customs wants originals or properly issued electronic versions accompanying the goods.

Common pitfalls that delay customs clearance

The most frequent failures are procedural: test reports issued for the wrong product group, labeling missing in Russian or Kazakh, a certificate that expired before the shipment arrived, or documents naming a legal entity different from the importer on file with customs.

All of these are avoided with one habit: agree the certification plan at contract stage. A factory that ships regularly to EAC markets — Hymebel holds 8 international certifications and has completed 1,000+ projects across 50+ countries — builds the paperwork into the production schedule, so documents and containers arrive together.

One pitfall deserves its own paragraph: ownership of the process. When the factory, the importer and a third-party agent each assume another holds the certificate, the shipment discovers the gap at the border. Assign the certification file to a named owner on both sides, review it at the same milestone as shop drawings, and the risk table above stays theoretical.

The same discipline carried our Hilton Tashkent delivery, where every carton arrived against a documented packing list — a habit that transfers directly to EAC markets, where documents and cargo are checked with equal attention. Regional buyers can verify this style of delivery on a named, international-brand reference rather than a promise.

Frequently asked questions

What is TR CU 025/2011 in simple terms?

It is the mandatory technical regulation on furniture safety for countries in the Eurasian Customs Union. It sets requirements for materials, strength and emissions, and makes EAC conformity documents a precondition for customs clearance.

Does furniture imported to Kazakhstan need an EAC certificate?

Yes. Kazakhstan is a Customs Union member, so imported furniture must carry an EAC certificate or declaration of conformity before customs clearance, supported by test reports from an accredited laboratory.

Does furniture shipped to Uzbekistan need EAC?

No — Uzbekistan is not an EAC member, and national import rules apply there. EAC documents are required for shipments to Customs Union members such as Kazakhstan.

What happens if furniture arrives without conformity documents?

The shipment is held at the border: storage charges, potential fines and a slipping opening schedule follow. That is why the certification plan must be completed before containers are loaded, not negotiated after arrival.
